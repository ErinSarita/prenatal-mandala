The Pregnancy and Birth Mandala

A teaching map of pregnancy and birth, read from the center out. The child is at the center, the new life growing within her mother. Around her is the mother. Around the mother is her circle of support, the people who hold her so she can hold her baby. Around them is the educator, who brings up possible imprints and best practices for each phase. On the outer petals are practices the mother can use to connect with her baby, and practices for healing, where she and her partner can explore their own earliest stories.

Due dates follow Naegele's rule: the first day of the last period plus 280 days (40 weeks). It is an estimate. Labor and birth can come any time from about 37 to 42 weeks, shown on the wheel as the birth window.

About this mandala

A mandala is a circle organized around a center. Traditions across the world have used it as a map: of the cosmos, of the seasons, of the stages of a life. This one maps the roughly forty weeks of pregnancy and the passage of birth through the lens of pre- and perinatal education, where the baby is understood as aware and taking in her world from the very beginning.

How it is built

The circle begins at the top, with the first day of the last period, and moves clockwise. Three quarters are the trimesters, three months each. The last quarter is birth: labor, the birth itself, and the first hour.

The rings line up month by month, so reading straight out from any slice shows, at the same moment, the baby, the mother, how her circle can hold her, and what the educator brings. The practices on the outer petals are gathered by trimester, for connecting with the baby and for the parents' own healing.

The rings are also layers of support: each holds the one inside it. Ray Castellino taught that everyone who gives support needs support too, and that the mother and the baby each need at least two layers (White, 2013). Who fills them is different for every family. What matters is that no one holds it all alone.

Around the petals, the time wheel holds every marker of time: the months, a petal for each of the forty weeks, and gold marks where each trimester and birth begin. Once a date is entered, it adds the due date, the birth window from 37 to 42 weeks, the full and new moons, and, if you like, the birth itself, actual or planned. Tap any month, week, or moon for its dates.

At the very edge, a braid of three strands, the baby, the mother, and the support around her, holds the mandala together.

When to share it

The mandala is best understood as a whole before pregnancy, and it can also be used alongside the pregnancy, before each phase unfolds. An educator might offer it in quarters: begin the first quarter as early as possible, and introduce each trimester shortly before it begins, around weeks 10 to 13 for the second and weeks 24 to 27 for the third. Share the birth quarter in the early to middle third trimester, around weeks 28 to 34, so the family has time to practice and plan well before the birth window opens at 37 weeks.

When the unexpected happens

Pregnancy and birth do not always go as planned. Whether a hard experience becomes trauma often depends less on what happened than on how it was lived through: whether the mother felt informed, supported, respected, and part of the decisions. The same turn of events can be held as overwhelming or as something met and moved through. Perception is shaped by support, so resilience can be built ahead of time. The educator ring carries these practices month by month, under Building resilience, at the point in the pregnancy where each one fits.

  • Prepare for flexibility, not fear. Exploring other paths, such as a longer labor, an induction, or a cesarean, lets a family meet a change as a choice rather than a collapse of the plan.
  • Keep her in the decisions. Knowing she can ask questions, take a moment, and say yes or no helps her stay the one choosing, even when plans change (MFCI).
  • Continuous support. A trusted partner, doula, or companion who stays with her through the unexpected is one of the strongest protections against a traumatic experience (Buckley; Seng and Taylor).
  • Tell the baby what is happening. Narrating a change, staying close when possible, and offering repair words afterward help the baby feel held through it too (Strange).
  • Rupture and repair. Hard moments, or ruptures, come into every relationship, including the one with the baby: a fearful reaction, a separation, an intervention. What matters most is the repair: returning, naming what happened, and reconnecting. A rupture that is repaired can build stronger trust than if it had never happened (Strange).
  • Make meaning afterward. Telling the story and being heard helps a hard experience settle into one that can be understood. When distress lingers, such as flashbacks, numbness, or avoidance, refer to trauma-informed or perinatal mental health support (Seng and Taylor).

The ten moons

Many traditions count a pregnancy as ten moons, and the forty weeks of pregnancy do span nine or ten full moons. Entering a date marks the real full and new moons of this pregnancy around the wheel, a fun way to follow the weeks of pregnancy alongside the moon's own cycle of about 29.5 days. The full moons are numbered, the new moons are the small dark circles between them, and if a birth entered falls past the due date, the moons of those extra weeks appear with a dashed outline. Tap or hover over a moon to see its date and how many weeks along the pregnancy is.

A note on language

The lines on what the baby may be sensing, and the possible imprints, are written with care. They draw on research and on the experience of the field's pioneers, and they describe possibilities. The imprints and questions for each month are ones that may begin to emerge then, not ones settled within that month. Every baby and every pregnancy is its own. Following Karen Strange, the baby is called she.
